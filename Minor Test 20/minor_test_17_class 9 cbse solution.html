SECTION A: SHORT CONCEPT SOLUTIONS (10 Marks)

Q1. State any two key postulates proposed by Niels Bohr that explained atomic stability.
[2 Marks]
Key Postulates of Bohr's Atomic Model:

1. Stationary Orbits/Energy Levels: Electrons revolve around the nucleus only in certain permitted, discrete circular orbits called stationary states or energy levels (labeled K, L, M, N or \(n=1, 2, 3, 4\)). [1 Mark]

2. Non-Radiative Motion: While revolving inside these allowed discrete orbits, the electron does not radiate or lose energy. Hence, the electron does not spiral into the nucleus, ensuring atomic stability. [1 Mark]

Q2. Define Atomic Number (\(Z\)) and Mass Number (\(A\)). Write the standard notation for Carbon (\(6p, 6n\)).
[2 Marks]

Atomic Number (\(Z\)): The total number of protons present inside the nucleus of an atom of an element. (\(Z = \text{Number of protons}\)) [0.5 Mark]

Mass Number (\(A\)): The total sum of the number of protons and neutrons (nucleons) present inside the nucleus of an atom. (\(A = p^+ + n^0\)) [0.5 Mark]

Standard Chemical Notation for Carbon:

For Carbon: Atomic Number \(Z = 6\), Mass Number \(A = 6 + 6 = 12\).

$$\mathbf{_{6}^{12}\text{C}}$$

[1 Mark]

SECTION B: SHORT ANSWER SOLUTIONS (15 Marks)

Q6. Describe Thomson's atomic model. Why was it discarded after the gold foil experiment?
[3 Marks]

Thomson's Model (Plum Pudding / Watermelon Model):

Proposed by J.J. Thomson in 1898. According to this model:

1. An atom consists of a sphere of positively charged matter. [0.5 Mark]

2. Negatively charged electrons are embedded uniformly throughout this positive sphere, like seeds in a watermelon or raisins in a plum pudding. [0.5 Mark]

Reason for Discarding Thomson's Model:

In Rutherford's \(\alpha\)-ray scattering experiment, a small fraction of \(\alpha\)-particles bounced back (\(180^\circ\) deflection) and many were scattered through large angles. Thomson's model predicted that positive charge was spread out thinly across the entire volume, which could never produce a concentrated electrostatic force strong enough to deflect fast-moving, heavy \(\alpha\)-particles. Thus, it failed to explain the existence of a dense positive nucleus. [2 Marks]

Q7. State Bohr-Bury rules for electron distribution. Calculate capacities for K, L, and M shells.
[3 Marks]

Bohr-Bury Rules:

Rule 1: The maximum number of electrons that can occupy a given shell is given by the formula \(2n^2\), where \(n\) is the shell number (orbit index). [1 Mark]

Rule 2: The outermost valence shell cannot hold more than 8 electrons, even if it has capacity for more. [0.5 Mark]

Rule 3: Electrons are filled stepwise in shells from inner to outer order (K \(\rightarrow\) L \(\rightarrow\) M). Inner shells must be filled completely before filling outer shells. [0.5 Mark]

Capacity Calculation (\(2n^2\)):

$$\text{K-shell } (n=1): 2 \times (1)^2 = \mathbf{2 \text{ electrons}}$$ $$\text{L-shell } (n=2): 2 \times (2)^2 = \mathbf{8 \text{ electrons}}$$ $$\text{M-shell } (n=3): 2 \times (3)^2 = \mathbf{18 \text{ electrons}}$$

[1 Mark]

Q8. Calculate the average atomic mass of Chlorine (\(75\%\) \(^{35}\text{Cl}\) and \(25\%\) \(^{37}\text{Cl}\)).
[3 Marks]

Step-by-Step Calculation:

Given Isotope 1: \(^{35}\text{Cl}\) with isotopic mass = \(35\text{ u}\) and abundance = \(75\%\).

Given Isotope 2: \(^{37}\text{Cl}\) with isotopic mass = \(37\text{ u}\) and abundance = \(25\%\).

Formula for Weighted Average Atomic Mass:

$$\text{Average Atomic Mass} = \left( \text{Mass}_1 \times \frac{\text{Abundance}_1}{100} \right) + \left( \text{Mass}_2 \times \frac{\text{Abundance}_2}{100} \right)$$

[1 Mark]

Substituting the given values:

$$\text{Average Atomic Mass} = \left( 35 \times \frac{75}{100} \right) + \left( 37 \times \frac{25}{100} \right)$$
$$\text{Average Atomic Mass} = \left( 35 \times \frac{3}{4} \right) + \left( 37 \times \frac{1}{4} \right)$$
$$\text{Average Atomic Mass} = \frac{105}{4} + \frac{37}{4} = \frac{142}{4} = \mathbf{35.5 \text{ u}}$$
Final Answer: 35.5 u

[2 Marks]

Q9. Define Valency. How is valency determined for Sodium (\(Z=11\)) and Oxygen (\(Z=8\))?
[3 Marks]

Valency: The combining capacity of an atom of an element, which is equal to the number of electrons lost, gained, or shared by the atom to complete its octet (8 electrons in valence shell, or 2 in duplet). [1 Mark]

(a) Sodium (\(Z=11\)):

• Electronic configuration = 2, 8, 1 (Valence electrons = 1).

• Since valence electrons \(\le 4\), Sodium readily loses 1 electron to achieve octet.

• Valency of Sodium = 1. [1 Mark]

(b) Oxygen (\(Z=8\)):

• Electronic configuration = 2, 6 (Valence electrons = 6).

• Since valence electrons \(> 4\), Oxygen gains 2 electrons to complete its octet.

• Valency = \(8 - \text{Valence electrons} = 8 - 6 = \mathbf{2}\). [1 Mark]

Q10. Element X has Mass Number = 35, Neutrons = 18. Find Z, configuration, identity, and valency.
[3 Marks]

(a) Atomic Number and Total Electrons:

We know, \(\text{Mass Number } (A) = \text{Protons } (Z) + \text{Neutrons } (n)\)

\(35 = Z + 18 \implies Z = 35 - 18 = \mathbf{17}\).

Since atom is neutral, Total Electrons = \(Z = \mathbf{17}\). [1 Mark]

(b) Electronic Configuration & Valence Electrons:

• Electronic Configuration (K, L, M) = 2, 8, 7.

• Number of Valence Electrons = 7. [1 Mark]

(c) Identity and Valency:

• Element with \(Z = 17\) is Chlorine (\(\text{Cl}\)).

• Valency = \(8 - 7 = \mathbf{1}\). [1 Mark]

SECTION C: LONG ANSWER SOLUTIONS (15 Marks)

Q11. Detailed observations, conclusions, and limitations of Rutherford's Gold Foil Experiment.
[5 Marks]

(a) & (b) Observations and Corresponding Conclusions:

Observation 1: Most of the high-energy \(\alpha\)-particles passed straight through the gold foil without any deflection. [0.5 Mark]

Conclusion 1: Most of the space inside an atom is completely empty. [0.5 Mark]

Observation 2: A few \(\alpha\)-particles were deflected from their straight path by small and large angles. [0.5 Mark]

Conclusion 2: The positive charge of the atom is not spread throughout, but concentrated in a very small volume at the center that repels positively charged \(\alpha\)-particles. [0.5 Mark]

Observation 3: Extremely few \(\alpha\)-particles (about 1 in 12,000) rebounded back along their original path (\(180^\circ\) deflection). [0.5 Mark]

Conclusion 3: All the positive charge and nearly all the mass of the gold atom are concentrated in an extremely tiny, dense region at the center called the nucleus. [0.5 Mark]

(c) Drawback/Limitation of Rutherford's Model:

According to classical electromagnetic theory, any charged particle (like an electron) revolving in a circular orbit undergoes continuous acceleration. An accelerating charged particle must continuously radiate energy. As a result, the revolving electron would lose energy continuously, its orbit would shrink spirally inward, and it would ultimately collapse into the nucleus within a fraction of a second. If this were true, matter could not exist stably. Rutherford's model could not explain this atomic stability. [2 Marks]

Part (b): Inert Nature of Noble Gases [2.5 Marks]

• Neon (\(Z=10\)): Electronic configuration = 2, 8 (Outer shell has 8 electrons = Complete Octet). [1 Mark]

• Argon (\(Z=18\)): Electronic configuration = 2, 8, 8 (Outer shell has 8 electrons = Complete Octet). [1 Mark]

• An atom attains maximum chemical stability when its outermost shell possesses a completely filled octet (8 electrons). Because Neon and Argon already possess stable octet configurations, they have zero tendency to gain, lose, or share electrons. Hence, their chemical combining capacity (valency) is zero and they are chemically inert. [0.5 Mark]
