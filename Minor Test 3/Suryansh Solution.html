State the Distributive Property of multiplication over addition for integers. Provide one mathematical example to illustrate it.
Solution:
Statement: For any three integers \(a\), \(b\), and \(c\), \(a \times (b + c) = (a \times b) + (a \times c)\).
[1 Mark]
Example: Let \(a = 2, b = 3, c = 4\).
LHS = \(2 \times (3 + 4) = 2 \times 7 = 14\)
RHS = \((2 \times 3) + (2 \times 4) = 6 + 8 = 14\). LHS = RHS.

2. Iron Ploughshares: Replaced wooden ploughshares, allowing deep ploughing of the heavy, clayey soil. This significantly increased agricultural yield, leading to surplus food production.

Iron weapons (swords, spears, arrowheads) were far superior to bronze/copper. Kings who controlled iron mines (like in Magadha) equipped their armies with strong weapons, giving them a decisive military advantage to conquer other Janapadas and build empires.

A merchant earns a profit of ₹15 per bag of basmati rice sold and a loss of ₹5 per bag of non-basmati rice sold.
(i) He sells 3,000 bags of basmati rice and 5,000 bags of non-basmati rice in a month. What is his net profit or loss?
(ii) What is the number of basmati rice bags he must sell to have neither profit nor loss, if the number of non-basmati rice bags sold is 6,400?
Solution:
Part (i):
Profit on 1 bag of basmati = +₹15
Total profit on 3,000 basmati bags = \(3000 \times 15 = \) ₹45,000
[1 Mark]
Loss on 1 bag of non-basmati = -₹5
Total loss on 5,000 non-basmati bags = \(5000 \times (-5) = \) -₹25,000
Net Profit/Loss = \(45,000 + (-25,000) = 20,000\)
Answer (i): The merchant makes a net profit of ₹20,000.
[1 Mark]
Part (ii):
Condition: Neither profit nor loss. This means Total Profit + Total Loss = 0, or Total Profit = Total Loss.
Number of non-basmati bags sold = 6,400.
Total loss incurred = \(6400 \times 5 = \) ₹32,000.
[1 Mark]
To cover this loss, the profit from basmati bags must be exactly ₹32,000.
Let the number of basmati bags be '\(x\)'.
\(15 \times x = 32,000 \implies x = \frac{32,000}{15}\)
[1 Mark]
\(x = 2133.33...\) bags.
Teacher's Note: Since bags cannot be sold in fractions, he must sell 2134 bags of basmati rice to ensure he does not suffer an overall loss (he will make a marginal profit of ₹10). (Mathematically exact answer: \(2133 \frac{1}{3}\) bags).

19.
The physical features of India heavily influence its economy and lifestyle. Justify this statement by explaining the economic importance and distinct geographical characteristics of the Peninsular Plateau. How do its rivers differ from those in the Northern Plains?
Solution:
Geographical Characteristics:
The Peninsular Plateau is a highly ancient, stable landmass formed from older crystalline, igneous, and metamorphic rocks (Gondwana land). It has a triangular shape with broad, shallow valleys and rounded hills. It is divided into the Central Highlands and the Deccan Plateau.
[1.5 Marks]
Economic Importance:
It acts as the "Storehouse of Minerals" of India. It is exceptionally rich in mineral resources like coal, iron ore, manganese, bauxite, etc. (especially in the Chota Nagpur plateau region). This wealth forms the backbone of India's metallurgical and heavy industries, generating immense employment and economic growth. Black soil areas (Deccan Trap) are highly suitable for cotton cultivation.
[2 Marks]
Difference in Rivers:
1. Water Source: Peninsular rivers (Godavari, Krishna, Narmada) are rain-fed and seasonal. Northern plains rivers (Ganga, Brahmaputra) are perennial (fed by Himalayan glaciers + rain).
2. Terrain & Navigation: Peninsular rivers flow through uneven, rocky terrain forming waterfalls; they are swift and largely unnavigable. Northern rivers flow sluggishly over flat terrain, making them highly suitable for navigation and extensive canal irrigation.
[1.5 Marks]
20.
Give a detailed account of the "Second Urbanisation" in ancient India. In your answer, include the reasons for the emergence of Mahajanapadas in the Ganga plains, the forms of government they practiced (Monarchy vs. Republic), and the role of punch-marked coins in expanding trade.
Solution:
1. Second Urbanisation & Emergence of Mahajanapadas (approx. 6th century BCE):
The shift of the Aryan civilization from the Indus Valley to the fertile Ganga plains marked this era. The primary catalyst was iron technology. Iron tools allowed vast forest tracts to be cleared, and deep ploughing generated large agricultural surpluses. This surplus supported non-agricultural populations (artisans, traders), leading to the rise of large cities and powerful territorial states called Mahajanapadas (e.g., Magadha, Kosala).
[2 Marks]
2. Forms of Government:
- Monarchies (Rajyas): Ruled by a single king (Raja) who held absolute power. Kingship was hereditary. E.g., Magadha.
- Republics (Gana-Sanghas): Ruled by a group or an assembly of elders/nobles, rather than a single king. Decisions were made by voting in assemblies. E.g., Vajji, Mallas.
[1.5 Marks]
3. Role of Punch-marked Coins:
Prior to this period, barter systems were prevalent. The introduction of punch-marked coins (mostly made of silver and copper with symbols punched onto them) standardized value exchange. This drastically simplified transactions, reduced the limitations of the barter system, and caused a tremendous boom in both internal and long-distance trade, firmly establishing a money economy.
[1.5 Marks]
