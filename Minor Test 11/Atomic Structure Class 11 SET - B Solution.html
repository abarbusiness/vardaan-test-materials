SECTION A
Q1. Define Isotopes and Isobars with one suitable example for each.
Isotopes: Atoms of the same element having the same atomic number but different mass numbers.
Example: Protium (\(^1_1H\)), Deuterium (\(^2_1H\)), and Tritium (\(^3_1H\)). [1 Mark]
Isobars: Atoms of different elements having different atomic numbers but the same mass number.
Example: Argon (\(^{40}_{18}Ar\)) and Calcium (\(^{40}_{20}Ca\)). [1 Mark]
Q2. State Planck's Quantum Theory and write its mathematical expression defining all terms.
Planck's Quantum Theory: It states that radiant energy is emitted or absorbed discontinuously in the form of small discrete packets of energy called 'quanta' (or 'photons' in the case of light). [1 Mark]
Mathematical Expression: \(E = h\nu\)
Where:
\(E\) = Energy of the quantum/photon
\(h\) = Planck's constant (\(6.626 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J s}\))
\(\nu\) = Frequency of the radiation [1 Mark]
Q3. Write the electronic configuration of \(Fe^{2+}\) and \(Fe^{3+}\) ions. Which is more stable?
Fe (Z=26) configuration: \([Ar] 4s^2 3d^6\)
\(Fe^{2+}\) configuration: \([Ar] 3d^6\) (electrons removed from 4s first) [0.5 Mark]
\(Fe^{3+}\) configuration: \([Ar] 3d^5\) [0.5 Mark]
Stability: \(Fe^{3+}\) is more stable than \(Fe^{2+}\) because it possesses an exactly half-filled d-subshell (\(3d^5\)), which provides extra stability due to high exchange energy and symmetrical distribution of electrons. [1 Mark]
Q4. Calculate the total number of angular nodes and radial nodes present in a \(3p\) orbital.
For a \(3p\) orbital: Principal quantum number \(n = 3\), Azimuthal quantum number \(l = 1\).
Angular nodes: Equal to \(l\) = 1. [1 Mark]
Radial nodes: Formula is \((n - l - 1)\) = \(3 - 1 - 1 = 1\). [1 Mark]
Q5. Define wave number. What is its relationship with wavelength and frequency?
Wave number (\(\bar{\nu}\)): It is defined as the number of wavelengths per unit length. It is the reciprocal of wavelength. [1 Mark]
Relationship:
With wavelength: \(\bar{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda}\)
With frequency: Since \(c = \nu\lambda \implies \lambda = \frac{c}{\nu}\), therefore \(\bar{\nu} = \frac{\nu}{c}\) [1 Mark]
SECTION B
Q6. State the \((n+l)\) rule. Arrange: \(4d, 5p, 5s, 6s\) in increasing order of energy.
\((n+l)\) Rule: Orbitals with a lower value of \((n+l)\) have lower energy. If two orbitals have the same \((n+l)\) value, the orbital with the lower value of \(n\) has lower energy. [1 Mark]
Calculating \((n+l)\) values:
\(5s: n=5, l=0 \implies 5+0 = 5\)
\(4d: n=4, l=2 \implies 4+2 = 6\)
\(5p: n=5, l=1 \implies 5+1 = 6\)
\(6s: n=6, l=0 \implies 6+0 = 6\) [1 Mark]
Increasing order of energy:
Lowest is \(5s\). Among \(4d, 5p, 6s\) (all have sum=6), order follows increasing \(n\).
Correct Order: \(5s < 4d < 5p < 6s\) [1 Mark]
Q7. Calculate wavelength of an electron moving with velocity \(2.05 \times 10^7 \text{ m/s}\).
Using de Broglie's equation: \(\lambda = \frac{h}{mv}\) [1 Mark]
Given: \(h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34} \text{ kg m}^2\text{s}^{-1}\), \(m = 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \text{ kg}\), \(v = 2.05 \times 10^7 \text{ m/s}\)
\[ \lambda = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}{(9.11 \times 10^{-31}) \times (2.05 \times 10^7)} \] [1 Mark]
\[ \lambda = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}{18.6755 \times 10^{-24}} \] \[ \lambda = 0.3548 \times 10^{-10} \text{ m} = 3.55 \times 10^{-11} \text{ m} \] [1 Mark]
Q8. Briefly explain line emission spectrum of hydrogen. Name the visible series.
Line Emission Spectrum of Hydrogen: When an electric discharge is passed through gaseous hydrogen, the \(H_2\) molecules dissociate and the energetically excited hydrogen atoms emit electromagnetic radiation of discrete frequencies. The hydrogen spectrum consists of several series of lines named after their discoverers. [2 Marks]
Visible Series: The Balmer series is the only spectral series of hydrogen that lies in the visible region of the electromagnetic spectrum. [1 Mark]
Q9. Calculate uncertainty in velocity. \(\Delta x = 0.1 \mathring{A}\).
Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle: \(\Delta x \cdot \Delta p \geq \frac{h}{4\pi}\) or \(\Delta x \cdot (m \Delta v) = \frac{h}{4\pi}\) [1 Mark]
Given: \(\Delta x = 0.1 \mathring{A} = 0.1 \times 10^{-10} \text{ m} = 10^{-11} \text{ m}\)
\[ \Delta v = \frac{h}{4\pi m \Delta x} \] \[ \Delta v = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}{4 \times 3.1416 \times 9.11 \times 10^{-31} \times 10^{-11}} \] [1 Mark]
\[ \Delta v = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34}}{114.47 \times 10^{-42}} \] \[ \Delta v = 0.05788 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s} = 5.79 \times 10^6 \text{ m/s} \] [1 Mark]
Q10. Threshold freq \(\nu_0 = 7.0 \times 10^{14}\). Incident \(\nu = 1.0 \times 10^{15}\). Calculate Kinetic Energy.
Einstein's Photoelectric Equation: \(K.E. = h\nu - h\nu_0 = h(\nu - \nu_0)\) [1 Mark]
Given: \(h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34} \text{ J s}\)
\(\nu = 1.0 \times 10^{15} \text{ s}^{-1} = 10 \times 10^{14} \text{ s}^{-1}\)
\(\nu_0 = 7.0 \times 10^{14} \text{ s}^{-1}\)
\[ K.E. = 6.626 \times 10^{-34} \times (10.0 \times 10^{14} - 7.0 \times 10^{14}) \] [1 Mark]
\[ K.E. = 6.626 \times 10^{-34} \times 3.0 \times 10^{14} \] \[ K.E. = 19.878 \times 10^{-20} \text{ J} = 1.988 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J} \] [1 Mark]
SECTION C
Q11. (a) Degenerate orbitals? (b) 4 Quantum numbers for 19th electron of K. (c) Max electrons for \(n=4, m_s=-1/2\).
(a) Degenerate Orbitals: Orbitals belonging to the same subshell of the same major energy level having exactly the same energy (in the absence of an external magnetic/electric field) are called degenerate orbitals.
Example: The three p-orbitals (\(p_x, p_y, p_z\)) of the 2p subshell are degenerate. [2 Marks]
(b) 19th electron of Potassium (Z=19):
Electronic configuration: \(1s^2 2s^2 2p^6 3s^2 3p^6 \mathbf{4s^1}\)
The 19th electron enters the \(4s\) orbital.
Principal Q.N. (\(n\)) = 4
Azimuthal Q.N. (\(l\)) = 0 (for s-orbital)
Magnetic Q.N. (\(m_l\)) = 0
Spin Q.N. (\(m_s\)) = \(+\frac{1}{2}\) (or \(-\frac{1}{2}\)) [2 Marks]
(c) Maximum electrons: For \(n=4\), the total number of orbitals is \(n^2 = 16\). Each orbital can hold one electron with spin \(-1/2\). Thus, maximum 16 electrons can have this set of quantum numbers. [1 Mark]
Q12. (a) Rydberg equation. (b) Max emission lines H atom \(n=6\) to ground. (c) Energy first orbit \(He^+\).
(a) Rydberg Equation:
\(\bar{\nu} = \frac{1}{\lambda} = R_H \left( \frac{1}{n_1^2} - \frac{1}{n_2^2} \right) Z^2\) [1 Mark]
(b) Maximum emission lines:
Formula for max lines when electron drops from \(n\) to ground state (\(n=1\)) is: \(\frac{n(n-1)}{2}\)
Here, \(n=6\). Max lines = \(\frac{6(6-1)}{2} = \frac{30}{2} = 15\) lines. [2 Marks]
(c) Energy of first orbit of \(He^+\):
Formula: \(E_n = -R_H \left( \frac{Z^2}{n^2} \right)\)
For \(He^+\) ion, \(Z=2\), and for the first orbit \(n=1\).
\[ E_1 = -2.18 \times 10^{-18} \times \left(\frac{2^2}{1^2}\right) \] \[ E_1 = -2.18 \times 10^{-18} \times 4 = -8.72 \times 10^{-18} \text{ J} \] [2 Marks]
Q13. (a) Macroscopic wave nature not observable? (b) Heisenberg principle. (c) Circumference = \(n\lambda\) derivation.
(a) Macroscopic wave nature: According to de Broglie, \(\lambda = \frac{h}{mv}\). Because the mass (\(m\)) of everyday macroscopic objects (like a cricket ball) is extremely large compared to Planck's constant (\(h\)), the associated wavelength (\(\lambda\)) becomes unimaginably small (negligible). Hence, their wave nature cannot be observed or measured. [2 Marks]
(b) Heisenberg's Uncertainty Principle: It is impossible to determine simultaneously, the exact position and exact momentum (or velocity) of a microscopic particle like an electron. [1 Mark]
(c) Circumference and de Broglie Wavelength:
According to Bohr's postulate of angular momentum: \(mvr = \frac{nh}{2\pi}\)
Rearranging the terms: \(2\pi r = \frac{nh}{mv}\)
We know de Broglie equation: \(\lambda = \frac{h}{mv}\)
Substituting \(\lambda\) in the rearranged Bohr equation:
\(2\pi r = n\lambda\)
Since \(2\pi r\) is the circumference of the circular orbit, this proves the circumference is an integral multiple (\(n\)) of the de Broglie wavelength. [2 Marks]
Q14. (a) Emission vs Absorption spectra. (b) 25W bulb, \(\lambda = 0.57 \mu\text{m}\), rate of emission.
(a) Difference:
- Emission Spectrum: Obtained when radiation from an excited substance is analyzed. It consists of bright lines on a dark background.
- Absorption Spectrum: Obtained when white light is passed through a substance and transmitted light is analyzed. It consists of dark lines on a continuous bright background. [2 Marks]
(b) Rate of emission of quanta:
Power of bulb (\(P\)) = \(25 \text{ J/s}\)
Wavelength (\(\lambda\)) = \(0.57 \mu\text{m} = 0.57 \times 10^{-6} \text{ m}\)
Energy of one photon (\(E\)) = \(\frac{hc}{\lambda} = \frac{6.626 \times 10^{-34} \times 3 \times 10^8}{0.57 \times 10^{-6}}\)
\(E = \frac{19.878 \times 10^{-26}}{0.57 \times 10^{-6}} = 3.487 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}\)
[1.5 Marks]
Rate of emission (number of photons per sec, \(n\)) = \(\frac{\text{Total Power}}{\text{Energy of 1 photon}}\)
\[ n = \frac{25}{3.487 \times 10^{-19}} = 7.17 \times 10^{19} \text{ s}^{-1} \] [1.5 Marks]
Q15. (a) Shapes of d-orbitals. (b) Significance of \(m_l\). (c) Continuous spectrum.
(a) Boundary surface diagrams of d-orbitals:
There are five d-orbitals: \(d_{xy}, d_{yz}, d_{zx}, d_{x^2-y^2}, d_{z^2}\).
- The first four have a cloverleaf (double dumbbell) shape with lobes lying between the axes or along the axes.
- The \(d_{z^2}\) orbital has a unique dumbbell shape with a circular collar (doughnut shape) in the xy-plane.
(Examiner notes: 2 marks for properly drawing and labeling any two d-orbital sketches). [2 Marks]
(b) Significance of Magnetic Quantum Number (\(m_l\)): It determines the spatial orientation of the orbital with respect to a standard set of coordinate axes. It basically tells us the number of orbitals in a given subshell. [1 Mark]
(c) Continuous Spectrum: A spectrum that contains all wavelengths (or frequencies) of visible light continuously merging into one another without any gap is called a continuous spectrum.
Example: A rainbow formed by the dispersion of sunlight through raindrops. [2 Marks]
