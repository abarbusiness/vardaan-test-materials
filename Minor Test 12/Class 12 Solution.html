6.
On the basis of hybridisation of the carbon bearing the halogen, distinguish between a haloalkane and a haloarene. Also state how monohalo-, dihalo- and polyhalogen compounds are classified.
Solution — Q.6

Haloalkane vs Haloarene — Hybridisation Distinction:

Haloalkane (sp³ C—X)
Haloarene (sp² C—X)
  • Haloalkane: X is bonded to an sp³-hybridised carbon. The carbon is tetrahedral, σ-bonded only. Examples: CH₃Cl, CH₃CH₂Br.
  • Haloarene: X is bonded to an sp²-hybridised carbon of the aromatic ring. The C—X bond has partial double bond character due to resonance. Examples: C₆H₅Cl, C₆H₅Br.

Classification by number of halogens:

  • Monohalo compounds: One halogen atom per molecule (e.g., CH₃Cl)
  • Dihalo compounds: Two halogen atoms per molecule (e.g., CH₂Cl₂)
  • Polyhalogen compounds: More than two halogen atoms per molecule (e.g., CHCl₃, CCl₄)
NCERT Reference: Chapter 6, Section 6.1, Page 199–200, Paragraphs 1 and 7 — "On the basis of the hybridisation of the carbon to which the halogen is attached, halocompounds can be classified as having sp³ C—X (haloalkanes) or sp² C—X (haloarenes/vinylic halides)." Also page 200 last para: classification as mono-, di-, and polyhalogen derivatives.

12.
Among the methyl halides CH₃F, CH₃Cl, CH₃Br and CH₃I, the one with the highest dipole moment is:
Solution — Q.12

Dipole moment (μ) = charge (q) × distance (d). For methyl halides, two competing factors govern μ:

  • Electronegativity of X → greater electronegativity → greater charge separation → increases μ. Order: F > Cl > Br > I
  • Bond length (C—X) → longer bond → increases d → increases μ. Order: C—I > C—Br > C—Cl > C—F

These two factors oppose each other. The net result from experimental data (NCERT Table 6.3):

Compound Dipole Moment (D) C—X Bond Length (pm)
CH₃F1.847 D139 pm
CH₃Cl1.860 D ← Highest178 pm
CH₃Br1.830 D193 pm
CH₃I1.636 D214 pm

CH₃Cl has the highest dipole moment because the decrease in electronegativity from F→Cl is compensated by the significant increase in bond length, making the product q×d maximum for CH₃Cl.

✔ Correct Answer: (b) CH₃Cl

NCERT Reference: Chapter 6, Section 6.3 "Nature of C—X Bond", Page 204, Table 6.3 — "Dipole moments of alkyl halides: CH₃F = 1.847 D, CH₃Cl = 1.860 D, CH₃Br = 1.830 D, CH₃I = 1.636 D. Despite F being most electronegative, CH₃Cl has the highest dipole moment due to the balance between electronegativity and bond length."

13.
Why is the carbon–halogen bond of an alkyl halide polarised? Show the partial charges on a general C—X bond, and explain why C—X bond length increases from C—F to C—I.
Solution — Q.13

Why C—X bond is polarised:

Halogens (F, Cl, Br, I) are all more electronegative than carbon. This difference in electronegativity causes the shared electron pair of the C—X bond to be drawn towards the halogen atom. As a result, the halogen develops a partial negative charge (δ−) and carbon develops a partial positive charge (δ+).

Polarity of C—X bond

Why C—X bond length increases C—F → C—I:

Bond length depends on the size of the atom and its atomic radius. As we move down Group 17 (F→Cl→Br→I), the atomic radius of the halogen increases due to addition of electron shells. The larger the halogen atom, the longer the bond formed with carbon:

C—F13964 (smallest)
C—Cl17899
C—Br193114
C—I214133 (largest)

16.
Why is thionyl chloride considered the best reagent for converting an alcohol into an alkyl chloride? Write the equation showing all products.
Solution — Q.16 (Thionyl Chloride Method)

Reaction:

Why SOCl₂ is the best reagent — Three reasons:

  1. Pure product: Both by-products (SO₂ and HCl) are gases and escape from the reaction mixture automatically. No separation needed.
  2. Clean reaction: Since no liquid or solid by-products are formed in the mixture, the alkyl chloride can be isolated in pure form by simply removing the gaseous by-products.
  3. No side reactions: Unlike PCl₅ (gives POCl₃ which must be separated) or HCl/ZnCl₂ (can cause rearrangements in 3° and some 2° alcohols), SOCl₂ gives a cleaner, more controlled reaction.

Overall advantage: The by-products being gaseous makes purification trivial — the product R—Cl is obtained directly pure.

NCERT Reference: Chapter 6, Section 6.4.1, Page 205, Paragraph 4 — "Thionyl chloride is preferred because the by-products (SO₂ and HCl) are gases and escape from the reaction vessel, leaving behind the pure alkyl chloride." This is one of the most important NCERT statements in Section 6.4.

17.
  1. Write the order of reactivity of 1°, 2° and 3° alcohols with a given haloacid.
  2. How is the reaction of a tertiary alcohol with concentrated HCl carried out, and how do conditions differ for 1° and 2° alcohols?
Solution — Q.17

(a) Reactivity Order of Alcohols with Haloacids:

The reaction of alcohols with HX (e.g., HCl) involves protonation of OH to form a good leaving group (H₂O), followed by nucleophilic attack by X⁻. The stability of the intermediate carbocation determines the rate:

3° alcohol > 2° alcohol > 1° alcohol

Tertiary carbocations (R₃C⁺) are most stable due to hyperconjugation and inductive effect of 3 alkyl groups → react fastest. Primary carbocations are least stable → slowest.

(b) Conditions for different alcohols with conc. HCl:

  • 3° Alcohol: Reacts immediately with concentrated HCl at room temperature without a catalyst. The tertiary carbocation forms readily. Example: (CH₃)₃COH + HCl → (CH₃)₃CCl + H₂O (instant)
  • 2° Alcohol: Requires anhydrous ZnCl₂ (Lucas reagent) as catalyst and moderate warming. ZnCl₂ coordinates with the OH, making it a better leaving group. Reaction is slower than 3°.
  • 1° Alcohol: Does not react with conc. HCl even in presence of ZnCl₂ at room temperature. Requires either HBr/H₂SO₄ (for bromide) or phosphorus reagents. The primary carbocation pathway is too slow.

Lucas Test: This difference in reactivity with ZnCl₂/HCl is used as the Lucas test to distinguish 1°, 2°, and 3° alcohols (turbidity appears immediately for 3°, after 5 min for 2°, and not at all for 1° at room temperature).

NCERT Reference: Chapter 6, Section 6.4.1, Page 205, Paragraph 2 — "The order of reactivity of alcohols with a given hydrogen halide is: tertiary > secondary > primary (3° > 2° > 1°). Tertiary alcohols react immediately with HCl, secondary alcohols require Lewis acid catalyst (ZnCl₂), and primary alcohols require H₃PO₄ or red P/HX."

18.
Free radical chlorination of alkanes is not a good laboratory method for preparing a pure monohaloalkane. Explain why, using photochemical chlorination of butane as example.
Solution — Q.18 (Free Radical Chlorination)

Reason 1 — Multiple positions of attack: Butane has two types of hydrogen: C1 (primary, 6H) and C2 (secondary, 4H). Cl• radical can abstract H from either position, giving two isomeric monochlorides: 1-chlorobutane (CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Cl) and 2-chlorobutane (CH₃CH₂CHClCH₃).

Reason 2 — Polychlorination: Once a monochlorobutane is formed, it can react with more Cl₂ to give dichlorides, trichlorides, etc. The reaction is not selective and cannot be stopped at the monosubstitution stage cleanly.

Reason 3 — Statistical and reactivity factors: The relative amounts of 1-chloro vs 2-chloro products depend on both the number of H atoms and the relative reactivity of 1° vs 2° H (2° H is slightly more reactive). This further complicates purification.

Products from butane:

  • 1-Chlorobutane (from C1 or C4 H) — primary
  • 2-Chlorobutane (from C2 or C3 H) — secondary
  • Plus di-, tri-, and polychlorides

Conclusion: A mixture of isomers is always obtained. Separation is difficult and expensive, making this method impractical for laboratory preparation of a single pure product.

NCERT Reference: Chapter 6, Section 6.4.2 "From Hydrocarbons (Halogenation)", Page 206, Paragraphs 1–3 — "Free radical chlorination of alkanes is not a good laboratory method because it gives a mixture of mono- and polychlorinated products, and separation of isomers is difficult. The method gives butane → 1-chlorobutane + 2-chlorobutane + polychlorides."

19.
  1. Propene reacts with HI to give two products, of which one predominates. Write both products, identify major, and name the rule.
  2. What happens when HBr adds to propene in presence of peroxide? Name the effect.
Solution — Q.19 (Addition to Alkenes — Markovnikov vs Anti-Markovnikov)

(a) Addition of HI to propene:

Markovnikov's Rule: In the addition of HX to an unsymmetrical alkene, the H attaches to the carbon bearing more hydrogens (or: the nucleophilic X attaches to the carbon bearing fewer hydrogens). This is because the more substituted carbocation (2°) is more stable than the less substituted (1°):

  • Major: CH₃CHI—CH₃ (2-iodopropane) — I goes to C2 (less H), forming more stable 2° carbocation intermediate
  • Minor: CH₃CH₂CH₂I (1-iodopropane) — I goes to C1, via less stable 1° carbocation

Rule name: Markovnikov's Rule (also called Markovnikov Addition)

(b) Addition of HBr to propene in presence of peroxide (Anti-Markovnikov addition):

Peroxide Effect (Kharasch Effect): In the presence of peroxides (ROOR), the reaction proceeds via a free radical mechanism. Peroxide generates Br• radicals. The Br• radical adds to the terminal carbon (C1, less substituted) to give the more stable secondary radical at C2, ultimately producing 1-bromopropane — the opposite of Markovnikov's product. This reversal is called the Peroxide Effect or Anti-Markovnikov addition. Note: This anti-Markovnikov addition works only for HBr (not HCl or HI) in the presence of peroxides.

34.
Haloalkanes are only very slightly soluble in water but dissolve readily in organic solvents. Explain both facts in terms of the energy of attractions broken and formed.
Solution — Q.34 (Solubility of Haloalkanes)

Why haloalkanes are only slightly soluble in water:

For dissolution to occur, the energy gained from forming new solute–solvent attractions must be sufficient to overcome the energy required to break existing solvent–solvent and solute–solute attractions.

  • Interactions to break: (a) Strong H-bonding in water (water–water H-bonds, ~20 kJ/mol each). (b) Dipole–dipole and London forces in haloalkane.
  • New interactions formed: Only dipole–dipole attractions between polar C—X and water (no H-bonds, since haloalkane has no N–H or O–H).
  • Result: Energy to break water–water H-bonds >> energy gained from haloalkane–water dipole interactions. The net energy change is unfavourable → very low solubility.

Why haloalkanes dissolve readily in organic solvents (like benzene, diethyl ether, acetone, CCl₄):

  • Organic solvents have London forces and mild dipole interactions similar to haloalkanes.
  • "Like dissolves like": The non-polar (or slightly polar) nature of organic solvents matches the relatively non-polar character of haloalkanes.
  • The energy required to break solute–solute and solvent–solvent interactions ≈ energy gained from solute–solvent interactions → dissolution is favourable.

60.
Aryl halides are extremely less reactive towards nucleophilic substitution. Explain all four reasons.
Solution — Q.60 (Low SN Reactivity of Aryl Halides)

Four Reasons for Low Nucleophilic Substitution Reactivity:

(i) Resonance — Partial double bond character of C—X:

In haloarenes, the lone pairs on the halogen are in conjugation (resonance) with the π-electrons of the benzene ring. This gives the C—X bond partial double bond character. Therefore, the C—X bond is stronger and shorter (169 pm in C₆H₅Cl vs 177 pm in CH₃Cl), making it much harder to break. The halogen is reluctant to leave.

(ii) Difference in hybridisation of C bearing X:

In haloalkane, C—X is on an sp³ carbon. In haloarene, C—X is on an sp² carbon of the aromatic ring. sp² carbons have greater s-character (33% vs 25%) → the bonding electrons are held closer to the nucleus → the C—X bond is shorter and stronger → less susceptible to nucleophilic attack.

(iii) Instability of the phenyl cation:

In SN1, the rate-determining step is formation of a carbocation. The phenyl cation (C₆H₅⁺) would be extremely unstable because: (a) the vacant orbital would be an sp² orbital at the ring carbon — this orbital is perpendicular to the π-system and cannot be stabilised by resonance with the ring, (b) it would be a highly strained, destabilised cation. Therefore, SN1 path is essentially impossible for aryl halides.

(iv) Electronic repulsion between nucleophile and π-electron cloud:

The benzene ring carries a delocalized π-electron cloud above and below the ring plane. Any incoming nucleophile (anion or lone pair donor) is repelled by this electron cloud, making approach to the ring carbon very difficult. This electrostatic repulsion prevents the nucleophile from reaching the C—X carbon.

NCERT Reference: Chapter 6, Section 6.7.2(1), Page 223, Paragraphs 1–4 — "Aryl halides are less reactive than alkyl halides towards nucleophilic substitution because: (1) resonance gives C—X partial double bond character, (2) sp² C holds electrons more tightly, (3) phenyl cation is too unstable for SN1, (4) π-electron cloud repels nucleophile." All four reasons are explicitly listed in NCERT Section 6.7.2.

Although chlorine is electron-withdrawing, it is ortho-, para-directing in EAS and at the same time deactivates the ring. Resolve this apparent contradiction.
Solution — Q.66 (Dual Role of Cl — Deactivating but o/p-Directing)

Chlorine has two opposing electronic effects on the benzene ring:

1. Inductive Effect (−I effect) — Electron Withdrawing:

  • Cl is more electronegative than C, so through the σ-bond framework, Cl withdraws electron density from the ring inductively (−I effect)
  • This makes the ring less electron-rich overall
  • The electrophile (E⁺) finds the ring less attractive → reaction is SLOWER → ring is DEACTIVATED
  • The −I effect is the dominant effect controlling reactivity

2. Mesomeric/Resonance Effect (+M effect) — Electron Donating to o/p positions:

  • Cl has lone pair electrons in a p-orbital that conjugates with the benzene ring π-system
  • This resonance (+M effect) pushes electron density into the ring, specifically at the ortho and para positions (as shown in resonance structures of chlorobenzene)
  • The ortho and para positions thus become relatively more electron-rich than the meta position
  • Electrophile preferentially attacks these richer positions → o/p products major → ring is o/p-DIRECTED
  • The +M effect controls orientation

Resolution of the contradiction:

Effect Nature Controls
−I (inductive) of ClElectron withdrawing overallREACTIVITY — ring deactivated (slower than benzene)
+M (resonance) of ClElectron donating to o/p via lone pairORIENTATION — o/p director

Conclusion: Cl deactivates the ring (due to −I > +M overall) but still directs to ortho/para (because +M effect specifically enriches those positions). The net effect is a deactivated o/p-director.

The deactivation is due to the stronger −I effect (inductive electron withdrawal). The o/p-direction is due to the +M effect (resonance donation of lone pair to o/p positions). These effects are independent: inductive controls reactivity, resonance controls orientation."

73.
How will you bring about the following conversions? (Any ten, each step with reagent and condition.)
Solution — Q.73 (All 15 Conversions — Complete Solutions)

(i) Ethanol → But-1-yne:

  • Step 1: C₂H₅OH + HBr/H₂SO₄ → C₂H₅Br (1-bromoethane)
  • Step 2: 2 C₂H₅Br + 2 Na → C₄H₁₀ (butane) [Wurtz] — no, need alkyne.
  • Better route: CH₃CH₂OH → CH₂=CH₂ (by dehydration with H₂SO₄/443K) → CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → BrCH₂CH₂Br → alc. KOH → CH≡CH (acetylene) → Na/NH₃ → HC≡CNa → + CH₃CH₂Br → HC≡C—CH₂CH₃ (but-1-yne)

Alternatively: CH₃CH₂OH → CH₃CHO (oxidation) — long route. Concise route: Ethanol → CH₂=CH₂ (H₂SO₄/453K) → CH₂BrCH₂Br (Br₂) → alc. KOH × 2 → HC≡CH → Na/liq.NH₃ → NaC≡CH + C₂H₅Br → HC≡CCH₂CH₃ (but-1-yne)

(ii) Ethane → Bromoethene:

  • Step 1: C₂H₆ + Br₂ → C₂H₅Br (free radical bromination, UV, 1-bromoethane)
  • Step 2: C₂H₅Br + alc. KOH → CH₂=CH₂ (elimination)
  • Step 3: CH₂=CH₂ + Br₂ → BrCH₂CH₂Br (addition)
  • Step 4: BrCH₂CH₂Br + alc. KOH → CH₂=CHBr (bromoethene)

(iii) Propene → 1-Nitropropane:

  • Step 1: CH₃CH=CH₂ + HBr (peroxide/anti-Markovnikov) → CH₃CH₂CH₂Br (1-bromopropane)
  • Step 2: CH₃CH₂CH₂Br + AgNO₂ → CH₃CH₂CH₂NO₂ (1-nitropropane)

(iv) Toluene → Benzyl alcohol:

  • Step 1: C₆H₅CH₃ + Cl₂ → C₆H₅CH₂Cl (benzyl chloride) [hν, free radical mechanism, side chain chlorination]
  • Step 2: C₆H₅CH₂Cl + NaOH(aq) → C₆H₅CH₂OH (benzyl alcohol)

(v) Propene → Propyne:

  • Step 1: CH₃CH=CH₂ + Br₂ → CH₃CHBrCH₂Br (1,2-dibromopropane)
  • Step 2: CH₃CHBrCH₂Br + 2 alc. KOH → CH₃C≡CH (propyne) [double dehydrohalogenation]

(vi) Ethanol → Ethyl fluoride:

  • Step 1: C₂H₅OH + HBr → C₂H₅Br (1-bromoethane)
  • Step 2: C₂H₅Br + AgF (heat) → C₂H₅F (ethyl fluoride) [Swarts reaction]

(vii) Bromomethane → Propanone:

  • Step 1: CH₃Br + KCN → CH₃CN (methyl cyanide/acetonitrile)
  • Step 2: CH₃CN + H₂O (H⁺) → CH₃COOH (acetic acid) — not propanone route.
  • Better: 2 CH₃Br + Na → CH₃CH₃ (Wurtz). Then CH₃CHO route... Let me think: CH₃Br → CH₃MgBr (Grignard, dry ether) → CH₃MgBr + CH₃CHO → CH₃CH(OH)CH₃ (sec. alcohol) → [O] → CH₃COCH₃ (propanone)

(viii) But-1-ene → But-2-ene:

  • Step 1: CH₃CH₂CH=CH₂ + HBr → CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Br (anti-Markovnikov, peroxide) — no, want but-2-ene.
  • Step 1: CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃ + HBr (Markovnikov) → CH₃CHBrCH₂CH₃ (2-bromobutane)
  • Step 2: CH₃CHBrCH₂CH₃ + alc. KOH → CH₃CH=CHCH₃ (but-2-ene) [Zaitsev product, major]

(ix) 1-Chlorobutane → n-Octane:

  • 2 CH₃CH₂CH₂CH₂Cl + 2 Na → CH₃(CH₂)₆CH₃ (n-octane) + 2 NaCl [Wurtz reaction, dry ether]

(x) Benzene → Biphenyl (diphenyl):

  • Step 1: C₆H₆ + Br₂ (FeBr₃) → C₆H₅Br (bromobenzene)
  • Step 2: 2 C₆H₅Br + 2 Na → C₆H₅—C₆H₅ (biphenyl) + 2 NaBr [Fittig reaction, dry ether]

(xi) Aniline → Chlorobenzene:

  • Step 1: C₆H₅NH₂ + NaNO₂ + HCl (273 K) → C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ (diazonium salt)
  • Step 2: C₆H₅N₂⁺Cl⁻ + Cu₂Cl₂/HCl → C₆H₅Cl (chlorobenzene) + N₂ [Sandmeyer's reaction]

(xii) tert-Butyl bromide → Isobutyl bromide:

  • Step 1: (CH₃)₃CBr + alc. KOH → (CH₃)₂C=CH₂ (isobutylene/2-methylpropene) [elimination]
  • Step 2: (CH₃)₂C=CH₂ + HBr (peroxide, anti-Markovnikov) → (CH₃)₂CHCH₂Br (isobutyl bromide)

(xiii) 2-Bromopropane → 1-Bromopropane:

  • Step 1: CH₃CHBrCH₃ + alc. KOH → CH₃CH=CH₂ (propene) [elimination]
  • Step 2: CH₃CH=CH₂ + HBr (peroxide) → CH₃CH₂CH₂Br (1-bromopropane) [anti-Markovnikov, peroxide effect]

(xiv) Chlorobenzene → p-Nitrophenol:

  • Step 1: C₆H₅Cl + HNO₃/H₂SO₄ → p-O₂N—C₆H₄—Cl (p-nitrochlorobenzene)
  • Step 2: p-O₂N—C₆H₄—Cl + NaOH (443 K, aq.) → p-O₂N—C₆H₄—ONa + NaCl
  • Step 3: p-O₂N—C₆H₄—ONa + HCl → p-O₂N—C₆H₄—OH (p-nitrophenol)

(xv) Aniline → Phenyl isocyanide (phenyl isonitrile):

  • C₆H₅NH₂ + CHCl₃ + 3 KOH (alc., heat) → C₆H₅NC (phenyl isocyanide) + 3 KCl + 3 H₂O [carbylamine reaction — isocyanide test for 1° amines]

74.
A hydrocarbon C₅H₁₀ does not react with chlorine in the dark but gives a single monochloro compound C₅H₉Cl in bright sunlight. Identify the hydrocarbon, draw its structure and justify both observations.
Solution — Q.74 (HOTS — Structure Deduction)

Analysis of clues:

  1. C₅H₁₀ → degree of unsaturation = (2×5+2−10)/2 = 1. The compound is either an alkene or a cycloalkane.
  2. "Does NOT react with Cl₂ in the dark" → alkenes react with Cl₂ in dark (ionic electrophilic addition). Therefore, it is NOT an alkene but a cycloalkane (cycloalkanes are saturated and only react via free radical substitution which requires light).
  3. "Gives a SINGLE monochloro compound in sunlight" → free radical halogenation gives only one monochloro product → all hydrogen atoms in the molecule are equivalent (identical/homotopic).

Identification: The only cycloalkane C₅H₁₀ where all H atoms are equivalent is cyclopentane.

Cyclopentane — all H atoms equivalent

Justification:

  • No reaction with Cl₂ in dark: Cyclopentane is a saturated cyclic compound with no C=C double bonds. Electrophilic addition (Cl₂ in dark, ionic mechanism) requires a π-bond. Since there is no C=C, Cl₂ does not add in the dark.
  • Single monochloro compound in sunlight: Cyclopentane has a regular pentagonal structure. All 10 H atoms are chemically equivalent (by symmetry, all CH₂ groups are identical). Therefore, free radical chlorination (requiring UV light) can only give one monochloro compound: chlorocyclopentane (C₅H₉Cl). There are no different types of H atoms, so no mixture of isomers is possible.

Reaction:

C₅H₁₀ + Cl₂ →(hν) C₅H₉Cl + HCl [chlorocyclopentane, single product]

All-equivalent H atoms give single chloride. This is the type of HOTS question that integrates Section 6.4.2 (halogenation mechanism) and Section 6.1 (cycloalkane classification). 76.
Among the isomeric alkanes of molecular formula C₅H₁₂, identify — with structures and grouping of equivalent hydrogens — the isomer that on photochemical chlorination yields (i) a single monochloride, (ii) three isomeric monochlorides, (iii) four isomeric monochlorides.
Solution — Q.76 (HOTS — Equivalent Hydrogens & Free Radical Chlorination)

C₅H₁₂ has three structural isomers: n-pentane, isopentane (2-methylbutane), and neopentane (2,2-dimethylpropane).

The number of monochlorides produced = number of distinct types of H atoms in the molecule.

(i) Single monochloride → All H atoms equivalent:

Neopentane = 2,2-dimethylpropane

Neopentane [C(CH₃)₄, 2,2-dimethylpropane] — All 12 H atoms are identical (all are on equivalent CH₃ groups). Only ONE monochloride possible: (CH₃)₃CCH₂Cl (1-chloro-2,2-dimethylpropane)

(ii) Three isomeric monochlorides → Three types of H atoms:

n-Pentane — 3 distinct types of H atoms

(ii) Three isomeric monochlorides → Three distinct types of H atoms:

n-Pentane [CH₃—CH₂—CH₂—CH₂—CH₃] — Has 3 distinct types of hydrogen atoms:

  • Type 1 (C1 & C5): Two equivalent terminal —CH₃ groups (6H) → gives 1-chloropentane
  • Type 2 (C2 & C4): Two equivalent internal —CH₂— groups (4H) → gives 2-chloropentane
  • Type 3 (C3): Central —CH₂— group (2H) → gives 3-chloropentane

Isomeric Monochlorides: 3 (1-chloropentane, 2-chloropentane, 3-chloropentane)

(iii) Four isomeric monochlorides → Four distinct types of H atoms:

Isopentane / 2-Methylbutane [(CH₃)₂CHCH₂CH₃] — Has 4 distinct types of hydrogen atoms, yielding 4 isomeric monochlorides:

  1. Substitution at isopropyl —CH₃ groups (6H) → 1-Chloro-2-methylbutane
  2. Substitution at C2 —CH— group (1H) → 2-Chloro-2-methylbutane
  3. Substitution at C3 —CH₂— group (2H) → 2-Chloro-3-methylbutane
  4. Substitution at terminal C4 —CH₃ group (3H) → 1-Chloro-3-methylbutane
